01 · 13TH – 15TH C.

Every pattern is from a place.

Cobalt geometry on the walls of the Alhambra — eight-pointed stars that anticipated Fedorov's 1891 proof of two-dimensional symmetry by five centuries.

03 · LATE 15TH – 17TH C.

In Iznik, the Ottomans rediscovered a tomato-coral red that no kiln has matched since — raised in slight relief on a tin-white ground, wrapping saz leaves and tulips.

05 · 1531 – PRESENT

In Puebla, Spanish maiolica met Indigenous ceramic skill and Chinese cobalt arriving on the Manila Galleon. A transpacific synthesis on every facade.

02 · 10TH C. – PRESENT

Across the Strait in Fez, master craftsmen — maâlems — chisel each polygon by hand from a fired slab. The only major tile tradition where design is achieved by cutting, not painting.

04 · 16TH C. – PRESENT

Portugal's national autobiography is told in glaze. Azulejo panels covered church interiors and palace facades — the cheapest mass medium of the early modern world.

06 · 1915 – 1935

In colonial Chiayi, Japanese-made industrial tiles bloomed with Han auspicious motifs — peony, pomegranate, peach — on the doorways of merchant families. Twenty years, then silence.

This atlas catalogs fourteen anchor traditions, each visually unmistakable and structurally distinct. Browse them as Exhibits grouped by lineage, as a numbered Index, or along a Timeline spanning the 10th century to today.

About

A living atlas of the world's tile.

Travel Tiles is a system that treats decorative tile as living heritage — not a pattern library. Every tile is generated from the grammar of a real tradition, carries its provenance, and can be fabricated into fired clay through the same pipeline a workshop uses.

It runs as three things at once: a playable atlas, a parametric design studio, and a physical-product pipeline — bound by one rule, that a tile should always be traceable to where it came from.

How this tile was generated

Each tile is composed by a parametric grammar derived from the source tradition's vocabulary — motif zones (core, edge, corner), symmetry group, and palette. The structural layer below is what becomes the 3D-printed mold; the visual layer above is what you see on screen.
